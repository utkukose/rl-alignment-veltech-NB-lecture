RLHF lab

Part A turns the weight of the Kullback-Leibler (KL) penalty on real outputs of the notebook and shows the optimum of the penalised objective. Part B names the stages of reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). Part C computes a Bradley-Terry preference and a penalised reward by hand. Parts D and E repeat two calculations of the lecture step by step: the penalised rewards and advantages of one batch, and the optimum of the penalised objective for three outputs. Part F weighs helpfulness against harmlessness on six requests.

Part A: The KL leash on real outputs

The candidates below are real outputs of the notebook: The most likely sentences of the fine-tuned model and every sentence that the reinforcement-learning runs produced outside the grammar. For each one the lab knows its probability under the reference model, its reward-model score and its gold reward. For a KL weight beta, the optimal policy is proportional to the reference probability times exp(reward-model score / beta). Lower beta and watch what it buys.

This is the optimum of the KL-regularised objective from the lecture, computed over the 392 candidates only, with their reference probabilities rescaled to add up to one. The reference model is the fine-tuned model, and beta is the weight of the KL penalty. The horizontal axis shows the logarithm of beta to base 10: -1 stands for 0.1 and 0 for 1. The dashed line, the share of probability on sentences of the grammar, is drawn at four times its value, so the height 4 means 100 percent.

Part D: Penalised rewards and advantages of one batch

The worked example of the lecture: four sentences of one batch of RLHF, each with its score from the reward model and its log-ratio against the fine-tuned model. The sliders set the KL weight \(\beta\), the four scores and the four log-ratios. Values are rounded to three decimals.

Continue in Colab, section 6: the same loop over 300 iterations of 64 sentences.

Part E: The optimum of the KL-regularised objective for three outputs

The worked example of the lecture: A reference policy writes three outputs, the reward model scores them, and the optimum of RLHF multiplies each reference probability by \(e^{r/\beta}\) and normalises. The sliders set the reference probabilities of the first two outputs, the three scores and \(\beta\). Lower \(\beta\) and watch the rare output with the highest score take over.

Continue in Colab, section 7: seven KL weights and reward hacking.

Part F: Helpful and harmless, one weight between them

The illustration of the lecture: six requests to an assistant, three harmless and three harmful, each with three possible answers, to comply, to give a careful partial answer or to refuse. Each answer has a helpfulness score and a harmlessness score between 0 and 1. The scores are invented for the illustration. The reward of an answer is \(r = (1 - w)\, r_{\mathrm{help}} + w\, r_{\mathrm{harm}}\), and the assistant gives the answer with the highest reward. The slider sets the weight \(w\) of harmlessness, and the figure counts the two kinds of failure for every weight.
