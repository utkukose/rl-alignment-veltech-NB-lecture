Value-based lab

Part A repeats a calculation of the lecture step by step: Monte Carlo against temporal differences on one sentence. Part B computes one update of Q-learning and one of SARSA (state, action, reward, state, action) by hand. Part C classifies methods as on-policy or off-policy, temporal difference, Monte Carlo or dynamic programming. Part D repeats a second calculation of the lecture step by step: one update of a small Q-network. Part E explores the deadly triad on Baird's counterexample.

Part A: Monte Carlo against temporal differences, episode by episode

The worked example of the lecture: The agent writes the same sentence of four tokens in every episode, and only the last step is rewarded. Monte Carlo moves the four estimates towards the return after each episode, and temporal difference (TD) learning moves each estimate towards the reward plus the estimate of the next state. The sliders set the step size, the final reward and the number of episodes. Values are rounded to four decimals.

Continue in Colab, section 2: both estimators over 3000 episodes and ten seeds.

Part B: One Q-learning and one SARSA update by hand

The current value is Q(s, a) = 2.0. The agent receives the reward -1 and lands in state s'. The best action in s' has the value 4.0, but the agent explores and will take an action whose value is 1.0. The discount factor is 0.9 and the step size 0.5.

Part D: One update of a small Q-network

A network with three inputs, two hidden units and two outputs, the Q-values of two actions, is small enough to follow by hand. It has the same structure as the network of the deep Q-network (DQN) of the lecture: a layer with the rectified linear unit (ReLU), a second layer, the target of Q-learning, the Huber loss, the gradients of both layers by backpropagation and one update with the Adam optimiser. The learning rate is larger than the 0.003 of the experiment, so that the change can be seen. The weights are
